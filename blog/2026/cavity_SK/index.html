The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Model via the Cavity Method

2 June 2026 | E. Malatesta

The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model [Sherrington and Kirkpatrick (1975)] is a paradigmatic model of spin glass theory. It is defined by the Hamiltonian

HN(σ)=−1N∑1≤i<j≤NJijσiσj , H_N(\sigma) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{1\le i < j\le N} J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j \,, HN​(σ)=−N​1​1≤i<j≤N∑​Jij​σi​σj​,

where σi∈{−1,+1}\sigma_i\in\{-1,+1\}σi​∈{−1,+1} are NNN binary spins. Each σi\sigma_iσi​ interacts with all the others N−1N-1N−1 spins with a coupling JijJ_{ij}Jij​ that is a extracted from a standard normal distribution

Jij∼N(0,1) J_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1) Jij​∼N(0,1)

The normalization 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​ in equation (1) makes the energy extensive. The partition function and the free energy density for a given realization of the coupling JijJ_{ij}Jij​ are

ZN(β)=∑σe−βHN(σ),fN(β)=−1βNlog⁡ZN(β). Z_N(\beta)=\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta H_N(\sigma)}, \qquad f_N(\beta)=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\log Z_N(\beta). ZN​(β)=σ∑​e−βHN​(σ),fN​(β)=−βN1​logZN​(β).

This model has been analyzed by using the replica method [Mézard, Parisi and Virasoro (1987)] a standard but heuristic approach in statistical physics. The replica method enables to compute the average free energy density in the large thermodynamic limit

f(β)≡lim⁡N→∞fN(β)‾ f(\beta) \equiv \lim_{N \to \infty} \overline{f_N(\beta)} f(β)≡N→∞lim​fN​(β)​

where by ∙‾\overline{\bullet}∙ we have denoted the average over the couplings. In brief the outcome of the replica computation gives an expression of f(β)f(\beta)f(β) in terms of some quantities (called order parameters) that satisfy some self-consistent equations that extremize f(β)f(\beta)f(β).

The goal of this post is to derive f(β)f(\beta)f(β) and those self-consistent equations using the cavity method [Mézard, Parisi and Virasoro (1987), Mézard, Parisi, Virasoro (1986)]. These equations coincide with those obtained by imposing a replica-symmetric (RS) ansatz within the replica method. We leave the derivation of the Replica-Symmetry-Breaking equations using the cavity method to later posts.

The word "cavity" refers to the following simple experiment: assume that the Gibbs measure of an NNN-spin system is known, add one more spin, and ask how the free energy changes. The new spin feels an effective random field generated by the old spins. If this field is described self-consistently, the usual RS equations appear without using the replica method.

Adding one spin

Start from an equilibrated system of NNN spins σ1 ,… ,σN\sigma_1\,, \dots\,, \sigma_Nσ1​,…,σN​. Next imagine to add a new spin σ0\sigma_0σ0​ and NNN couplings J0k∼N(0,1)J_{0k} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)J0k​∼N(0,1), k∈[N]k \in [N]k∈[N] connecting σ0\sigma_0σ0​ to σ1 ,… ,σN\sigma_1\,, \dots\,, \sigma_Nσ1​,…,σN​, see the figure below.

Figure 1: The cavity construction. A new spin σ0\sigma_0σ0​ is added to an equilibrated NNN-spin system and connected to the old spins through new independent couplings J0kJ_{0k}J0k​.

For the moment I ignore a subleading normalization issue in the couplings, and write the (N+1)(N+1)(N+1)-spin Hamiltonian as

HN+1(σ,σ0)=HN(σ)−σ0h0(σ), H_{N+1}(\sigma,\sigma_0) = H_N(\sigma)-\sigma_0 h_0(\sigma), HN+1​(σ,σ0​)=HN​(σ)−σ0​h0​(σ),

where

h0cav(σ)=1N∑k=1NJ0kσk h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^N J_{0k}\sigma_k h0cav​(σ)=N​1​k=1∑N​J0k​σk​

is the instantaneous cavity field acting on the new spin. The superscript cav{\rm cav}cav stresses that this field is measured in the system where the new spin is still absent. Note also that the couplings J0kJ_{0k}J0k​ are random variables, independent of the old system.

Using (5), the partition function of the N+1N+1N+1 spin system can be written as

ZN+1=∑σ,σ0e−βHN(σ)+βσ0h0cav(σ)=∑σe−βHN(σ)2cosh⁡ ⁣(βh0cav(σ))=ZN⟨2cosh⁡ ⁣(βh0cav(σ))⟩N. \begin{split} Z_{N+1} &= \sum_{\sigma,\sigma_0} e^{-\beta H_N(\sigma)+\beta\sigma_0 h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma)} \\ &= \sum_{\sigma} e^{-\beta H_N(\sigma)} 2\cosh\!\left(\beta h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma)\right) \\ &= Z_N \left\langle 2\cosh\!\left(\beta h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma)\right) \right\rangle_N. \end{split} ZN+1​​=σ,σ0​∑​e−βHN​(σ)+βσ0​h0cav​(σ)=σ∑​e−βHN​(σ)2cosh(βh0cav​(σ))=ZN​⟨2cosh(βh0cav​(σ))⟩N​.​

Here ⟨∙⟩N\langle \bullet\rangle_N⟨∙⟩N​ denotes the Gibbs average in the original NNN-spin system. Therefore the free-energy shift produced by the new spin is

ΔF=FN+1−FN=−1βlog⁡⟨2cosh⁡ ⁣(βh0cav(σ))⟩N. \Delta F = F_{N+1}-F_N = -\frac{1}{\beta} \log \left\langle 2\cosh\!\left(\beta h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma)\right) \right\rangle_N. ΔF=FN+1​−FN​=−β1​log⟨2cosh(βh0cav​(σ))⟩N​.

This equation is exact for the cavity Hamiltonian. The problem has now been reduced to understanding the distribution of the random field h0cav(σ)h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma)h0cav​(σ) under the Gibbs measure of the old NNN spin system :

Pcav(h)≡⟨δ(h−h0cav(σ))⟩N=1ZN∑σe−βHN(σ) δ(h−h0cav(σ)) \begin{split} P^{\rm cav}(h) &\equiv \left\langle \delta(h - h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma)) \right\rangle_N \\ &= \frac{1}{Z_N} \sum_{\sigma} e^{-\beta H_N(\sigma)} \, \delta(h - h^{\rm cav}_0(\sigma)) \end{split} Pcav(h)​≡⟨δ(h−h0cav​(σ))⟩N​=ZN​1​σ∑​e−βHN​(σ)δ(h−h0cav​(σ))​

This is what we call cavity field distribution.

Cavity Field Inside A State

By definition, the cavity field distribution depends on the properties of the Gibbs measure of the NNN spin system. Let us imagine the general situation in which the Gibbs measure decomposes into pure states α\alphaα,

ZN=∑αZα,N,Zα,N=e−βFα,N . Z_N=\sum_\alpha Z_{\alpha,N}, \qquad Z_{\alpha,N}=e^{-\beta F_{\alpha,N}}\,. ZN​=α∑​Zα,N​,Zα,N​=e−βFα,N​.

We now condition the Gibbs measure on one such state. Inside state α\alphaα, before adding σ0\sigma_0σ0​, define the cavity magnetizations

mk→0α=⟨σk⟩α,N∖0, m_{k\to 0}^\alpha = \langle\sigma_k\rangle_{\alpha,N}^{\setminus 0}, mk→0α​=⟨σk​⟩α,N∖0​,

qα=1N∑k=1N(mk→0α)2. q_\alpha = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^N (m_{k \to 0}^\alpha)^2. qα​=N1​k=1∑N​(mk→0α​)2.

When NNN is large, by the central limit theorem we should expect that the cavity field distribution is Gaussian. We therefore need to compute the first moment and the variance of the cavity field conditioned to state α\alphaα. The mean value of the cavity field in state α\alphaα is

u0α≡⟨h0cav(σ)⟩α,N∖0=1N∑k=1NJ0kmk→0α. u_0^\alpha \equiv \left\langle h_0^{\rm cav}(\sigma)\right\rangle_{\alpha,N}^{\setminus 0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^N J_{0k}m_{k\to 0}^\alpha. u0α​≡⟨h0cav​(σ)⟩α,N∖0​=N​1​k=1∑N​J0k​mk→0α​.

I will call u0αu_0^\alphau0α​ the mean cavity field, or cavity bias. This is the field to which the new spin responds once the old system is conditioned on the state α\alphaα.

Remind that, inside a pure state, connected correlations satisfy the clustering property, that is

lim⁡N→∞1N2∑i,j⟨(σi−miα)(σj−mjα)⟩α,N2=0. \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i,j} \left\langle (\sigma_i-m_i^\alpha)(\sigma_j-m_j^\alpha) \right\rangle_{\alpha,N}^2 =0. N→∞lim​N21​i,j∑​⟨(σi​−miα​)(σj​−mjα​)⟩α,N2​=0.

Using this property, we can compute the variance of the cavity field inside the state

⟨(h0cav−u0α)2⟩α,N=1N∑k,ℓJ0kJ0ℓ⟨(σk−mk→0α)(σℓ−mℓ→0α)⟩α,N≃1N∑k=1NJ0k2[1−(mk→0α)2]→1−qα. \begin{split} \left\langle \left(h_0^{\rm cav}-u_0^\alpha\right)^2 \right\rangle_{\alpha,N} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k,\ell} J_{0k}J_{0\ell} \left\langle (\sigma_k-m_{k \to 0}^\alpha)(\sigma_\ell-m_{\ell\to 0}^\alpha) \right\rangle_{\alpha,N} \\ &\simeq \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N J_{0k}^2 \left[ 1-(m_{k \to 0}^\alpha)^2 \right] \\ &\to 1-q_\alpha. \end{split} ⟨(h0cav​−u0α​)2⟩α,N​​=N1​k,ℓ∑​J0k​J0ℓ​⟨(σk​−mk→0α​)(σℓ​−mℓ→0α​)⟩α,N​≃N1​k=1∑N​J0k2​[1−(mk→0α​)2]→1−qα​.​

Therefore the cavity-field distribution inside state α\alphaα is

Pαcav(h)=12π(1−qα)exp⁡[−(h−u0α)22(1−qα)]. P^{\rm cav}_\alpha(h) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-q_\alpha)}} \exp\left[ -\frac{(h-u_0^\alpha)^2}{2(1-q_\alpha)} \right]. Pαcav​(h)=2π(1−qα​)​1​exp[−2(1−qα​)(h−u0α​)2​].

Equivalently,

h0cav=u0α+1−qα z,z∼N(0,1), h_0^{\rm cav}=u_0^\alpha+\sqrt{1-q_\alpha}\,z, \qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1), h0cav​=u0α​+1−qα​​z,z∼N(0,1),

Plugging this distribution into (8), and restricting to the state α\alphaα, gives

ΔFα=−1βlog⁡∫dh Pαcav(h) 2cosh⁡(βh)=−1βlog⁡∫Dz 2cosh⁡(βu0α+β1−qαz)=−β2(1−qα)−1βlog⁡2cosh⁡(βu0α). \begin{split} \Delta F_\alpha &= -\frac{1}{\beta} \log \int dh \, P^{\rm cav}_\alpha (h) \, %\frac{d h}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-q_\alpha)}} %\exp\left[ %-\frac{(h-h_\alpha)^2}{2(1-q_\alpha)} %\right] 2\cosh(\beta h) \\ &= -\frac{1}{\beta} \log \int Dz \, 2\cosh \left(\beta u_0^{\alpha} + \beta \sqrt{1-q_\alpha} z \right) \\ &= -\frac{\beta}{2}(1-q_\alpha) -\frac{1}{\beta} \log 2\cosh(\beta u_0^\alpha). \end{split} ΔFα​​=−β1​log∫dhPαcav​(h)2cosh(βh)=−β1​log∫Dz2cosh(βu0α​+β1−qα​​z)=−2β​(1−qα​)−β1​log2cosh(βu0α​).​

having denoted by DzDzDz a standard normal Gaussian measure

Cavity vs local field

It is useful to distinguish this cavity field from the local field felt by σ0\sigma_0σ0​ in the (N+1)(N+1)(N+1)-spin system at equilibrium. Before adding σ0\sigma_0σ0​, the field distribution in state α\alphaα is Gaussian as in (16). After adding σ0\sigma_0σ0​, configurations are reweighted by eβhσ0e^{\beta h\sigma_0}eβhσ0​. Therefore the joint distribution of the field value hhh and the spin σ0\sigma_0σ0​ in the enlarged system is

Pαloc(h,σ0)=1ZαPαcav(h)eβhσ0, P_\alpha^{\rm loc}(h,\sigma_0) = \frac{1}{\mathcal Z_\alpha} P_\alpha^{\rm cav}(h) e^{\beta h\sigma_0}, Pαloc​(h,σ0​)=Zα​1​Pαcav​(h)eβhσ0​,

where

Zα=∑σ0=±1∫dh Pαcav(h)eβhσ0=eβ22(1−qα) 2cosh⁡(βu0α). \mathcal Z_\alpha = \sum_{\sigma_0=\pm 1} \int dh\,P_\alpha^{\rm cav}(h)e^{\beta h\sigma_0} = e^{\frac{\beta^2}{2}(1-q_\alpha)}\,2\cosh(\beta u_0^\alpha). Zα​=σ0​=±1∑​∫dhPαcav​(h)eβhσ0​=e2β2​(1−qα​)2cosh(βu0α​).

After summing over σ0\sigma_0σ0​, we obtain the local-field distribution

Pαloc(h)=1ZαPαcav(h)2cosh⁡(βh). P_\alpha^{\rm loc}(h) = \frac{1}{\mathcal Z_\alpha} P_\alpha^{\rm cav}(h) 2\cosh(\beta h). Pαloc​(h)=Zα​1​Pαcav​(h)2cosh(βh).

Notice that the local field distribution is not Gaussian! Indeed, the local field is correlated with σ0\sigma_0σ0​: when σ0\sigma_0σ0​ is added, it influences the spins σ1 ,… ,σN\sigma_1\,,\dots\,,\sigma_Nσ1​,…,σN​, which in turn modify the field felt by σ0\sigma_0σ0​ itself. This feedback is called the Onsager reaction, discussed in the next section.

By contrast, the cavity field is measured in the system where the new spin is absent.

The Magnetization Inside A State and the TAP equations

We can now compute the magnetization of the newly added spin σ0\sigma_0σ0​ in state α\alphaα and when the N+1N+1N+1 system is equilibrated:

m0α=⟨σ0⟩α,N+1=∑σ0=±1∫dh Pαloc(h,σ0) σ0=∫dh Pαcav(h) 2sinh⁡(βh)∫dh Pαcav(h) 2cosh⁡(βh)=tanh⁡(βu0α). \begin{split} m^\alpha_0 &= \langle \sigma_0 \rangle_{\alpha, N+1} = \sum_{\sigma_0=\pm1}\int dh\,P_\alpha^{\rm loc}(h,\sigma_0)\,\sigma_0\\ &= \frac{\int dh\,P_\alpha^{\rm cav}(h)\,2\sinh(\beta h)}{\int dh\,P_\alpha^{\rm cav}(h)\,2\cosh(\beta h)} \\ &= \tanh(\beta u_0^\alpha). \end{split} m0α​​=⟨σ0​⟩α,N+1​=σ0​=±1∑​∫dhPαloc​(h,σ0​)σ0​=∫dhPαcav​(h)2cosh(βh)∫dhPαcav​(h)2sinh(βh)​=tanh(βu0α​).​

Thus the magnetization is determined by the mean cavity field u0αu_0^\alphau0α​, not by the average local field in the equilibrated (N+1)(N+1)(N+1)-spin system.

H0α≡1N∑k=1NJ0kmkα, H_0^\alpha \equiv %\left\langle h_0^{\rm cav}(\sigma)\right\rangle_{\alpha,N+1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k=1}^N J_{0k}m_k^\alpha, H0α​≡N​1​k=1∑N​J0k​mkα​,

where now

denotes the magnetization in the enlarged system. This is not the same object as u0αu_0^\alphau0α​, because u0αu_0^\alphau0α​ uses the cavity magnetizations mk→0αm_{k\to0}^\alphamk→0α​ of the system in which spin 000 is absent. Using the tilted distribution (20), the average local field can also be written as

H0α=∑σ0=±1∫dh Pαloc(h,σ0) h=∑σ0=±1∫dh Pαcav(h)eβhσ0h∑σ0=±1∫dh Pαcav(h)eβhσ0=∑σ0=±1eβu0ασ0∫Dz eβu0α1−qα σ0z(u0α+1−qα z)∑σ0=±1eβu0ασ0∫Dz eβ1−qα σ0z. \begin{split} H_0^\alpha &= \sum_{\sigma_0=\pm1}\int dh\,P_\alpha^{\rm loc}(h,\sigma_0)\,h \\ &= \frac{ \sum_{\sigma_0=\pm1} \int dh\,P_\alpha^{\rm cav}(h)e^{\beta h\sigma_0}h }{ \sum_{\sigma_0=\pm1} \int dh\,P_\alpha^{\rm cav}(h)e^{\beta h\sigma_0} } \\ &= \frac{ \sum_{\sigma_0=\pm1} e^{\beta u_0^\alpha \sigma_0} \int Dz\,e^{\beta u_0^\alpha \sqrt{1-q_\alpha}\, \sigma_0 z} \left(u_0^\alpha+\sqrt{1-q_\alpha}\,z\right) }{ \sum_{\sigma_0=\pm1} e^{\beta u_0^\alpha \sigma_0} \int Dz\,e^{\beta \sqrt{1-q_\alpha}\, \sigma_0 z} }. \end{split} H0α​​=σ0​=±1∑​∫dhPαloc​(h,σ0​)h=∑σ0​=±1​∫dhPαcav​(h)eβhσ0​∑σ0​=±1​∫dhPαcav​(h)eβhσ0​h​=∑σ0​=±1​eβu0α​σ0​∫Dzeβ1−qα​​σ0​z∑σ0​=±1​eβu0α​σ0​∫Dzeβu0α​1−qα​​σ0​z(u0α​+1−qα​​z)​.​

m0α=tanh⁡ ⁣[β(H0α−β(1−qα)m0α)]. m_0^\alpha = \tanh\!\left[ \beta\left( H_0^\alpha-\beta(1-q_\alpha)m_0^\alpha \right) \right]. m0α​=tanh[β(H0α​−β(1−qα​)m0α​)].

Written for a generic spin iii, this gives the TAP equations [Thouless, Anderson, Palmer (1977)]

miα=tanh⁡ ⁣[β(1N∑j≠iJijmjα−β(1−qα)miα)]. \boxed{ m_i^\alpha = \tanh\!\left[ \beta\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{j\ne i}J_{ij}m_j^\alpha - \beta(1-q_\alpha)m_i^\alpha \right) \right]. } miα​=tanh⎣⎡​β⎝⎛​N​1​j=i∑​Jij​mjα​−β(1−qα​)miα​⎠⎞​⎦⎤​.​

The key feature is the extra field correction

hons,iα=−β(1−qα)miα, h_{\rm ons,i}^\alpha = -\beta(1-q_\alpha)m_i^\alpha, hons,iα​=−β(1−qα​)miα​,

called the Onsager reaction term. It corrects naive mean-field theory by accounting for the effect of spin iii back on itself through the rest of the system.

The Single-State Assumption

Replica symmetry corresponds, in this cavity language, to the assumption that there is only one relevant thermodynamic state. We can then drop the label α\alphaα from the local magnetizations.

The cavity bias becomes

u0=1N∑k=1NJ0kmk→0. u_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^N J_{0k}m_{k\to0}. u0​=N​1​k=1∑N​J0k​mk→0​.

For fixed cavity magnetizations mk→0m_{k\to0}mk→0​, this quantity fluctuates only because of the new couplings J0kJ_{0k}J0k​. By the central limit theorem,

u0∼N(0,q),q=1N∑k=1Nmk→02. u_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,q), \qquad q=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^N m_{k\to0}^2. u0​∼N(0,q),q=N1​k=1∑N​mk→02​.

This statement concerns the mean cavity field u0u_0u0​, not the instantaneous cavity field h0cav(σ)h_0^{\rm cav}(\sigma)h0cav​(σ). Inside the state, we remind the latter has the distribution

h0cav(σ)=u0+1−q z. h_0^{\rm cav}(\sigma)=u_0+\sqrt{1-q}\,z. h0cav​(σ)=u0​+1−q​z.

We can now derive a self-consistent equation for the overlap. Since the new spin must be statistically equivalent to the old ones in the thermodynamic limit, its squared magnetization must reproduce the overlap qqq. Using (23),

m0=tanh⁡(βu0), m_0=\tanh(\beta u_0), m0​=tanh(βu0​),

and averaging over the Gaussian cavity bias u0=q zu_0=\sqrt q\,zu0​=q​z gives

q=∫Dz tanh⁡2 ⁣(βq z). \boxed{ q = \int D z\, \tanh^2\!\left(\beta\sqrt{q}\,z\right). } q=∫Dztanh2(βq​z).​

This is the RS self-consistency equation for the overlap that can be also obtained through the replica method.

The RS Free Energy

There is one final technical point to discuss. This will in turn allow us to connect the free-energy shift to the free energy of the model.

In the true SK Hamiltonian (1) with N+1N+1N+1 spins, all couplings should be normalized by 1/N+11/\sqrt{N+1}1/N+1​, while in the cavity construction above they were normalized by 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​. This difference can be absorbed by evaluating the (N+1)(N+1)(N+1)-spin partition function at the slightly rescaled inverse temperature. The free energy shift we have computed is therefore equal to

ΔF=−1βlog⁡ZN+1(βN+1N)+1βlog⁡ZN(β)=−1βlog⁡ZN+1(β)+1βlog⁡ZN(β)−12N1ZN+1∂ZN+1∂β \begin{split} \Delta F &= - \frac{1}{\beta} \log Z_{N+1}\left(\beta \sqrt{\frac{N+1}{N}}\right) + \frac{1}{\beta} \log Z_{N}(\beta) \\ &= - \frac{1}{\beta} \log Z_{N+1}\left(\beta \right) + \frac{1}{\beta} \log Z_{N}(\beta) - \frac{1}{2N} \frac{1}{Z_{N+1}} \frac{\partial Z_{N+1}}{\partial \beta} \end{split} ΔF​=−β1​logZN+1​(βNN+1​​)+β1​logZN​(β)=−β1​logZN+1​(β)+β1​logZN​(β)−2N1​ZN+1​1​∂β∂ZN+1​​​

Taking the average over the couplings this gives the identity

ΔF‾=f+12∂(βf)∂β=12β2∂∂β[β3f(β)]. \overline{\Delta F} = f + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial (\beta f)}{\partial \beta} = \frac{1}{2\beta^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta} \left[ \beta^3 f(\beta) \right]. ΔF=f+21​∂β∂(βf)​=2β21​∂β∂​[β3f(β)].

At low temperature a non-zero solution appears. This RS low-temperature saddle is not the full solution of the SK model: below the spin-glass transition the correct equilibrium measure requires replica-symmetry breaking. Still, the single-state cavity computation is the cleanest way of seeing where the RS equations come from and what their order parameter means.
