Chaos in Random Neural Networks

9 August 2026 | E. Malatesta

← All blog posts

I recently went back to the classic paper of Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers on chaos in random neural networks [Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers (1988)], which was revisited in detail in [Crisanti and Sompolinsky (2018)].

The model they considered is a recurrent neural network with random asymmetric couplings. Its remarkable feature is that, using Dynamical Mean Field Theory (DMFT), one can show that in the large-NNN limit the collective dynamics reduces to an effective one-neuron stochastic problem. The noise in this effective problem is generated self-consistently by the activity of the rest of the network.

In this first post I want to derive this reduction and the resulting closed equation for the autocorrelation function. I will then show how, in this model, the effective one-neuron problem can be recast as Newton's equation for a fictitious particle moving in a self-consistent potential. This mechanical analogy gives a very transparent way of organizing the formal DMFT solutions: static solutions, periodic solutions, and the special decaying solution associated with the chaotic phase.

The model

Consider NNN continuous variables hi(t)h_i(t)hi​(t) i=1,…,Ni=1,\dots,Ni=1,…,N, representing the post-synaptic potentials of NNN neurons. The activity, or output, of neuron iii is

Si(t)=ϕ(ghi(t)). S_i(t)=\phi(g h_i(t)). Si​(t)=ϕ(ghi​(t)).

Here ggg is the gain and ϕ\phiϕ is an odd sigmoidal-shaped function. I will mostly keep the notation general, but the canonical example I will consider is ϕ(x)=tanh⁡x\phi(x)=\tanh xϕ(x)=tanhx. The dynamics of the post-synaptic potentials are given by the following NNN coupled differential equations

dhidt=−hi(t)+1N∑j=1NJijSj(t). \frac{d h_i}{dt} = -h_i(t)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^N J_{ij}S_j(t). dtdhi​​=−hi​(t)+N​1​j=1∑N​Jij​Sj​(t).

The first term is the leak: without any input, hi(t)h_i(t)hi​(t) decays exponentially to zero. The second term is the recurrent input from the rest of the network, which depends on the coupling matrix JijJ_{ij}Jij​. Here we will consider random, independent, asymmetric couplings, having mean 0 and variance 1:

EJ[Jij]=0,EJ[Jij2]=1,∀i≠j=1,…,N \mathbb E_J[J_{ij}]=0, \qquad \mathbb E_J[J_{ij}^2]=1, \qquad \forall i \ne j = 1, \dots, N %J_{ij}\ \text{independent of}\ J_{ji}. EJ​[Jij​]=0,EJ​[Jij2​]=1,∀i=j=1,…,N

We also set Jii=0J_{ii}=0Jii​=0, which is irrelevant at large NNN. The scaling 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​ in equation (2) maintains each recurrent input of order one as N→∞N\to\inftyN→∞.

The configuration where all the neurons are silent

hi=0∀i h_i=0 \qquad \forall i hi​=0∀i

is always a fixed point. Linearizing around it gives

∂thi=−hi+gN∑jJijhj. \partial_t h_i = -h_i+\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_j J_{ij}h_j. ∂t​hi​=−hi​+N​g​j∑​Jij​hj​.

Since the eigenvalues of J/NJ/\sqrt{N}J/N​ fill the unit disk in the complex plane at large NNN, the rightmost real part of the spectrum of gJ/NgJ/\sqrt{N}gJ/N​ is ggg. Hence the zero fixed point is linearly stable for g<1g<1g<1 and loses stability at g=1g=1g=1. This simple calculation identifies the transition point, but it does not tell us what happens for g>1g>1g>1. For that we need the nonlinear mean-field theory.

Dynamical Mean Field Theory

Define the recurrent input to neuron iii:

ηi(t)≡1N∑j=1NJij ϕ(ghj(t)). \eta_i(t) \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^N J_{ij} \, \phi(g h_j(t)). ηi​(t)≡N​1​j=1∑N​Jij​ϕ(ghj​(t)).

The equation of motion becomes

∂thi(t)=−hi(t)+ηi(t). \partial_t h_i(t)=-h_i(t)+\eta_i(t). ∂t​hi​(t)=−hi​(t)+ηi​(t).

The idea of dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) is that, at large NNN, ηi(t)\eta_i(t)ηi​(t) becomes a Gaussian process. In the following we will perform a simple, naive computation, which gives the right equations.

At fixed trajectories {hj(t)}\{h_j(t)\}{hj​(t)}, ηi(t)\eta_i(t)ηi​(t) is a sum of many independent random variables. Its mean and covariance are:

EJ[ηi(t)]=0 ,EJ[ηi(t)ηi(t′)]=1N∑j=1Nϕ(ghj(t))ϕ(ghj(t′))≡C(t,t′). \begin{split} \mathbb E_J[\eta_i(t)]&=0 \,, \\ \mathbb E_J[\eta_i(t)\eta_i(t')] %&= %\sum_{j,k} \mathbb E_J[J_{ij}J_{ik}] S_j(t)S_k(t') \\ &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \phi(g h_j(t)) \phi(g h_j(t')) \equiv C(t, t'). \end{split} EJ​[ηi​(t)]EJ​[ηi​(t)ηi​(t′)]​=0,=N1​j=1∑N​ϕ(ghj​(t))ϕ(ghj​(t′))≡C(t,t′).​

At large NNN, the empirical average appearing in the covariance self-averages and converges to a deterministic two-time autocorrelation function C(t,t′)C(t,t')C(t,t′). The original NNN-dimensional network can therefore be reduced to a single effective neuron driven by a Gaussian input η(t)\eta(t)η(t) with covariance

⟨η(t)η(t′)⟩=C(t,t′) . \boxed{ \langle \eta(t)\eta(t')\rangle=C(t,t')\,.} ⟨η(t)η(t′)⟩=C(t,t′).​

Here and in the following, ⟨∙⟩\langle\bullet\rangle⟨∙⟩ denotes the average over the effective Gaussian process. The dynamics of the effective neuron is therefore

∂th(t)=−h(t)+η(t) \boxed{ \partial_t h(t)=-h(t)+\eta(t) } ∂t​h(t)=−h(t)+η(t)​

The covariance of the effective noise must be determined self-consistently from the activity of the effective neuron via

C(t,t′)=⟨ϕ(gh(t))ϕ(gh(t′))⟩. \boxed{ C(t,t') = \left\langle \phi(g h(t))\phi(g h(t')) \right\rangle. } C(t,t′)=⟨ϕ(gh(t))ϕ(gh(t′))⟩.​

Thus, the original dynamics of the NNN dimensional network has been replaced by a by a stochastic single-neuron problem whose noise statistics are generated self-consistently by the neuron itself. This is the central dynamical mean-field equation.

A small subtlety is hidden in this argument. The trajectories hj(t)h_j(t)hj​(t) are generated by the same coupling matrix JJJ, so the factors ϕ(ghj(t))\phi(g h_j(t))ϕ(ghj​(t)) cannot literally be regarded as fixed independently of the couplings JijJ_{ij}Jij​. The reason the conclusion is nevertheless correct is the full asymmetry and mean-field scaling of the couplings and can be understood with a cavity argument, see the footnote[1]. The same result can be derived more systematically using the Martin–Siggia–Rose–Janssen–De Dominicis (MSRJD) generating-functional formalism; see [Crisanti and Sompolinsky (2018)]. For a pedagogical introduction to this method in the context of mean-field spin-glass dynamics, see [Castellani and Cavagna (2005)].

The autocorrelation equation

We now focus on a stationary state where two-time quantities like the activity autocorrelation C(t,t′)C(t, t')C(t,t′) depend only on the time difference τ=t−t′\tau=t-t'τ=t−t′

C(τ)=C(t−t′) C(\tau) = C(t - t') C(τ)=C(t−t′)

Similarly the post-synaptic field autocorrelation

Δ(τ)=⟨h(t′)h(t′+τ)⟩,Δ0≡Δ(0). \Delta(\tau) = \left\langle h(t')h(t'+\tau)\right\rangle, \qquad \Delta_0 \equiv \Delta(0). Δ(τ)=⟨h(t′)h(t′+τ)⟩,Δ0​≡Δ(0).

Next, let's take the effective equation (1+∂t)h(t)=η(t)(1+\partial_t)h(t)=\eta(t)(1+∂t​)h(t)=η(t), multiply the left and the right hand side by η(t′)\eta(t')η(t′) and take the average with respect to η\etaη:

(1+∂t)(1+∂t′)⟨h(t)h(t′)⟩=⟨η(t)η(t′)⟩. (1+\partial_t)(1+\partial_{t'}) \left\langle h(t)h(t')\right\rangle = \left\langle \eta(t)\eta(t')\right\rangle. (1+∂t​)(1+∂t′​)⟨h(t)h(t′)⟩=⟨η(t)η(t′)⟩.

The right and left hand side contain by definition repsectively C(τ)C(\tau)C(τ) and Δ(τ)\Delta(\tau)Δ(τ). Using the fact that ∂t=∂τ\partial_t=\partial_\tau∂t​=∂τ​ and ∂t′=−∂τ\partial_{t'}=-\partial_\tau∂t′​=−∂τ​ one finds that the first order derivative cancel, obtaining

Δ(τ)−∂τ2Δ(τ)=C(τ) \boxed{ \Delta(\tau)-\partial_\tau^2\Delta(\tau)=C(\tau) %\partial_{\tau}^2 \Delta(\tau) = -C(\tau) + \Delta(\tau). } Δ(τ)−∂τ2​Δ(τ)=C(τ)​

This is yet not enough, as we need to express CCC self-consistently in terms of Δ\DeltaΔ. The key simplification is that h(t)h(t)h(t) is Gaussian. Indeed since the effective equation (10) is linear in hhh the solution can be written as

h(t)=∫−∞tds e−(t−s)η(s). h(t)=\int_{-\infty}^t ds\, e^{-(t-s)}\eta(s). h(t)=∫−∞t​dse−(t−s)η(s).

This is a linear functional of a Gaussian process, hence it is itself a Gaussian process. Therefore the pair (h(t),h(t+τ)) (h(t),h(t+\tau))(h(t),h(t+τ)) on which Δ(τ)\Delta(\tau)Δ(τ) depends is a two-dimensional Gaussian vector. Its covariance matrix is

Σ(τ)=(Δ0Δ(τ)Δ(τ)Δ0). \Sigma(\tau) = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta_0 & \Delta(\tau) \\ \Delta(\tau) & \Delta_0 \end{pmatrix}. Σ(τ)=(Δ0​Δ(τ)​Δ(τ)Δ0​​).

A two-dimensional Gaussian is completely fixed by this covariance matrix. Therefore C(τ)C(\tau)C(τ) is a function of Δ(τ)\Delta(\tau)Δ(τ) and Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​:

C(τ)=F(Δ(τ);Δ0). \boxed{ C(\tau)=F(\Delta(\tau);\Delta_0). } C(τ)=F(Δ(τ);Δ0​).​

We can represent the two correlated Gaussian variables using three independent standard Gaussians x,y,zx,y,zx,y,z:

h=Δ0−∣Δ∣ x+∣Δ∣ z ,h′=Δ0−∣Δ∣ y+sign(Δ)∣Δ∣ z. \begin{split} %h&=\sqrt{\Delta_0-\Delta}\,x+\sqrt{\Delta}\,z, \\ %h'&=\sqrt{\Delta_0-\Delta}\,y+\sqrt{\Delta}\,z. h&=\sqrt{\Delta_0-|\Delta|}\,x+\sqrt{|\Delta|}\,z \,, \\ h'&=\sqrt{\Delta_0-|\Delta|}\,y+\text{sign}(\Delta)\sqrt{|\Delta|}\,z. \end{split} hh′​=Δ0​−∣Δ∣​x+∣Δ∣​z,=Δ0​−∣Δ∣​y+sign(Δ)∣Δ∣​z.​

Then

⟨h2⟩=⟨h′2⟩=Δ0,⟨hh′⟩=Δ. \langle h^2\rangle=\langle h'^2\rangle=\Delta_0, \qquad \langle hh'\rangle=\Delta. ⟨h2⟩=⟨h′2⟩=Δ0​,⟨hh′⟩=Δ.

So we find, using the fact that ϕ\phiϕ is an odd function

F(Δ;Δ0)=sign(Δ)∫Dz[∫Dx ϕ(gΔ0−∣Δ∣ x+g∣Δ∣ z)]2. F(\Delta;\Delta_0) = \mathrm{sign}({\Delta})\int Dz \left[ \int Dx\, \phi\left( g\sqrt{\Delta_0-|\Delta|}\,x + g\sqrt{ |\Delta|}\,z \right) \right]^2. F(Δ;Δ0​)=sign(Δ)∫Dz[∫Dxϕ(gΔ0​−∣Δ∣​x+g∣Δ∣​z)]2.

where we have introduced the notation Dx≡dx2πe−x2/2Dx\equiv \frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}Dx≡2π​dx​e−x2/2. The closed DMFT equation is therefore

Δ(τ)−Δ¨(τ)=F(Δ(τ);Δ0). \boxed{ \Delta(\tau)-\ddot\Delta(\tau) = F(\Delta(\tau);\Delta_0). } Δ(τ)−Δ¨(τ)=F(Δ(τ);Δ0​).​

The unknown is a single function Δ(τ)\Delta(\tau)Δ(τ), and all dependence on the nonlinear transfer function is hidden in the scalar function FFF.

Properties of Δ(τ)\Delta(\tau)Δ(τ)

The equation (15) can be inverted explicitly, using the face that the Green function of the operator 1−∂τ21-\partial_\tau^21−∂τ2​ is

G(τ)=12e−∣τ∣, G(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|\tau|}, G(τ)=21​e−∣τ∣,

as (1−∂τ2)G(τ)=δ(τ)(1-\partial_\tau^2)G(\tau)=\delta(\tau)(1−∂τ2​)G(τ)=δ(τ). Therefore we find

Δ(τ)=12∫−∞+∞dτ′ e−∣τ−τ′∣C(τ′). \boxed{ \Delta(\tau) = \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} d\tau'\, e^{-|\tau-\tau'|} C(\tau'). } Δ(τ)=21​∫−∞+∞​dτ′e−∣τ−τ′∣C(τ′).​

This formula is useful because it gives the properties of Δ\DeltaΔ directly. First, Δ\DeltaΔ is an even function Δ(−τ)=Δ(τ)\Delta(-\tau)=\Delta(\tau)Δ(−τ)=Δ(τ) (the same is of course true for CCC). Secondly, Δ\DeltaΔ is differentiable function with zero derivative in the origin[2]

Δ˙(0)=0. \boxed{ \dot\Delta(0)=0. } Δ˙(0)=0.​

Third, since Δ\DeltaΔ is an autocorrelation, it obeys the Cauchy-Schwarz bound

∣Δ(τ)∣=∣⟨h(t)h(t+τ)⟩∣≤⟨h(t)2⟩⟨h(t+τ)2⟩=Δ0. |\Delta(\tau)| = |\langle h(t)h(t+\tau)\rangle| \le \sqrt{\langle h(t)^2\rangle\langle h(t+\tau)^2\rangle} = \Delta_0. ∣Δ(τ)∣=∣⟨h(t)h(t+τ)⟩∣≤⟨h(t)2⟩⟨h(t+τ)2⟩​=Δ0​.

Thus acceptable solutions must satisfy

∣Δ(τ)∣≤Δ0. \boxed{ |\Delta(\tau)|\le \Delta_0. } ∣Δ(τ)∣≤Δ0​.​

Collecting everything, the steady-state DMFT solutions are curves Δ(τ)\Delta(\tau)Δ(τ) satisfying

Δ(0)=Δ0,Δ˙(0)=0,∣Δ(τ)∣≤Δ0. \Delta(0)=\Delta_0, \qquad \dot\Delta(0)=0, \qquad |\Delta(\tau)|\le \Delta_0. Δ(0)=Δ0​,Δ˙(0)=0,∣Δ(τ)∣≤Δ0​.

The initial condition Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​ must be chosen so that the resulting solution is self-consistent.

The Newtonian particle analogy

Much insight can be gained into the problem by rewriting the DMFT equation (22) as

d2Δdτ2=Δ−F(Δ;Δ0)=−∂V∂Δ \frac{d^2 \Delta}{d \tau^2} = \Delta-F(\Delta;\Delta_0) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial \Delta} dτ2d2Δ​=Δ−F(Δ;Δ0​)=−∂Δ∂V​

where we have defined the potential[3]

V(Δ;Δ0)=−Δ22+∫0Δdu F(u;Δ0). \boxed{ V(\Delta;\Delta_0) = -\frac{\Delta^2}{2} + \int_0^\Delta du\,F(u;\Delta_0). } V(Δ;Δ0​)=−2Δ2​+∫0Δ​duF(u;Δ0​).​

The DMFT equation therefore turns out to be a Newton's equation for a particle with position Δ\DeltaΔ, moving in the time variable τ\tauτ, in the potential VVV starting at position Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​ with zero velocity. The energy

E=12Δ˙2+V(Δ;Δ0) \boxed{ E = \frac{1}{2}\dot\Delta^2+V(\Delta;\Delta_0) } E=21​Δ˙2+V(Δ;Δ0​)​

is conserved. The important point is that the potential itself depends on Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​. By changing Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​ we are not only changing the initial point of the Newtonian particle; we are also changing the shape of the potential in which it moves. We will see how the potential changes very concretely in the next section.

Therefore the problem is self-consistent in a very concrete mechanical sense. We must choose a starting point Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​, build the potential V(⋅;Δ0)V(\cdot;\Delta_0)V(⋅;Δ0​), release the particle from rest at Δ=Δ0\Delta=\Delta_0Δ=Δ0​, and then check whether the orbit is an admissible autocorrelation.

The shape of the potential

In order to classify the possible solutions to the DMFT equation (29), it is useful to understand how the potential can look like vs Δ\DeltaΔ, for each chosen initial condition Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​.

Start noticing that for an odd transfer function, such as ϕ(x)=tanh⁡x\phi(x)=\tanh xϕ(x)=tanhx, the function F(Δ;Δ0)F(\Delta;\Delta_0)F(Δ;Δ0​) is odd in Δ\DeltaΔ. Therefore the potential V(Δ;Δ0)V(\Delta; \Delta_0)V(Δ;Δ0​) in (30) is an even function of Δ\DeltaΔ. Hence it is enough to study its shape when Δ>0\Delta>0Δ>0.

In the next discussion we will argue that the potential cannot have an arbitrary shape. We can see this by computing the first three derivatives of the potential. Using the fact illustrated in the footnote[3], it is easy to show that, for Δ>0\Delta>0Δ>0

∂V∂Δ=−Δ+F(Δ;Δ0), \frac{\partial V}{\partial \Delta} = -\Delta+F(\Delta;\Delta_0), ∂Δ∂V​=−Δ+F(Δ;Δ0​), ∂2V∂Δ2=−1+∂F∂Δ=g2∫Dz[∫Dx ϕ′(gΔ0−Δ x+gΔ z)]2−1 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \Delta^2} = -1+ \frac{\partial F}{\partial \Delta} = g^2 \int Dz \left[ \int Dx\, \phi'\left( g\sqrt{\Delta_0-\Delta}\,x + g\sqrt{\Delta}\,z \right) \right]^2 - 1 ∂Δ2∂2V​=−1+∂Δ∂F​=g2∫Dz[∫Dxϕ′(gΔ0​−Δ​x+gΔ​z)]2−1 ∂3V∂Δ3=∂2F∂Δ2=g4∫Dz[∫Dx ϕ′′(gΔ0−Δ x+gΔ z)]2>0. \frac{\partial^3 V}{\partial \Delta^3} = \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \Delta^2} = g^4 \int Dz \left[ \int Dx\, \phi''\left( g\sqrt{\Delta_0-\Delta}\,x + g\sqrt{\Delta}\,z \right) \right]^2 >0. ∂Δ3∂3V​=∂Δ2∂2F​=g4∫Dz[∫Dxϕ′′(gΔ0​−Δ​x+gΔ​z)]2>0.

Therefore ∂Δ2V\partial^2_\Delta V∂Δ2​V is a monotonically increasing function of Δ\DeltaΔ for Δ>0\Delta>0Δ>0. This is a rather strong constraint: the curvature can change sign at most once. As a consequence the potential can only have few qualitative shapes. We can distinguish between them by looking at the sign of the curvature of the potential at the origin. The first possibility is

∂2V∂Δ2(0;Δ0)≥0. \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \Delta^2}(0;\Delta_0)\ge0. ∂Δ2∂2V​(0;Δ0​)≥0.

Since the curvature is increasing for Δ>0\Delta>0Δ>0, the potential is convex. Because VVV is even, Δ=0\Delta=0Δ=0 is then the unique minimum. The potential is a single well. The second possibility is

∂2V∂Δ2(0;Δ0)<0. \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \Delta^2}(0;\Delta_0)<0. ∂Δ2∂2V​(0;Δ0​)<0.

Then Δ=0\Delta=0Δ=0 is a local maximum. Since the curvature can change sign at most once, the potential can bend upward at only once in the physical interval Δ0>Δ>Δ\Delta_0 > \Delta > \DeltaΔ0​>Δ>Δ. If ∂ΔV\partial_{\Delta} V∂Δ​V crosses zero before the endpoint Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​, this crossing gives a unique minimum at positive Δ\DeltaΔ, and by evenness another minimum at negative Δ\DeltaΔ. In that case the potential has the shape of a double well.

If ∂ΔV\partial_{\Delta} V∂Δ​V does not cross zero before the endpoint Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​, then we have a downhill potential as it is monotonically decreasing in the whole interval Δ0>Δ>Δ\Delta_0 > \Delta > \DeltaΔ0​>Δ>Δ. As shown in the footnote[4] this case only happens when g≤1g\le 1g≤1, which implies that in this regime the DMFT equation admits only the solution Δ=0\Delta=0Δ=0.

Thus the monotonicity of ∂Δ2V\partial_\Delta^2 V∂Δ2​V tells us that only very simple shapes are possible: a single well centered at the origin, a double well, or a downhill potential on the admissible interval.

The boundary between the single-well and double-well shapes given by the condition

g∫Dx ϕ′(gΔ0 x)=1 \boxed{g \int Dx\, \phi'\left(g\sqrt{\Delta_0}\,x\right) = 1} g∫Dxϕ′(gΔ0​​x)=1​

In the case of the tanh⁡\tanhtanh function, for small Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​, the integral is close to one, so if g>1g>1g>1 the curvature at the origin is positive and the potential is a single well. As Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​ increases, the Gaussian variable gΔ0xg\sqrt{\Delta_0}xgΔ0​​x explores the saturated part of tanh⁡\tanhtanh, where ϕ′=sech⁡2\phi' = \operatorname{sech}^2ϕ′=sech2 is small. The integral decreases, the curvature at the origin eventually becomes negative, and the potential turns into a double well.

Static solutions

The simplest possible solution of the DMFT equation corresponds to an autocorrelation function independent of time

Δ(τ)=Δ0. \Delta(\tau)=\Delta_0. Δ(τ)=Δ0​.

This corresponds to the particle not moving and sitting at the stationary points of the potential. This gives the condition

Δ0=F(Δ0;Δ0)=∫Dz ϕ(gΔ0 z)2. \boxed{ \Delta_0=F(\Delta_0;\Delta_0) = \int Dz\,\phi(g\sqrt{\Delta_0}\,z)^2.} Δ0​=F(Δ0​;Δ0​)=∫Dzϕ(gΔ0​​z)2.​

For ϕ(x)=tanh⁡x\phi(x)=\tanh xϕ(x)=tanhx, Δ0=0\Delta_0=0Δ0​=0 is always a solution. This is the silent fixed point of the original network. The argument of the previous subsection show in the footnote[4] shows that for g≤1g\le1g≤1 this is the only admissible stationary DMFT solution. For g>1g>1g>1 equation (39) has also a nonzero solution Δ0>0\Delta_0>0Δ0​>0.

Time-dependent solutions

We here analyze other solutions of the DMFT equation (29) for g>1g>1g>1 which are time-dependent. Since the motion conserves energy, at each time the energy stays equal to the initial one corresponding to the one of the particle being at rest at Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​

E=V(Δ0;Δ0). E=V(\Delta_0;\Delta_0). E=V(Δ0​;Δ0​).

Different shapes of the potential lead to different types of formal DMFT solutions. If the initial condition Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​ is such that V(Δ0;Δ0)≠0V(\Delta_0; \Delta_0) \ne 0V(Δ0​;Δ0​)=0 then the autocorrelation diplays an oscillatory, periodic profile. This corresponds to a limit cycle in the original network. Moreover if V(Δ0;Δ0)>0V(\Delta_0; \Delta_0) > 0V(Δ0​;Δ0​)>0, then the Δ(τ)\Delta(\tau)Δ(τ) changes sign (when the particle reaches Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0). If instead V(Δ0;Δ0)<0V(\Delta_0; \Delta_0) < 0V(Δ0​;Δ0​)<0, then the particle is confined into the positive well of the potential, and the oscillations do not change sign.

There is also a special solution that separates those two regimes when

V(Δ0;Δ0)=0. \boxed{ V(\Delta_0;\Delta_0)=0. } V(Δ0​;Δ0​)=0.​

This solution decays to zero when for τ\tauτ large

Δ(τ)→0asτ→∞. \Delta(\tau)\to0 \qquad \text{as}\qquad \tau\to\infty. Δ(τ)→0asτ→∞.

This suggests that the underlying neural dynamics is chaotic, as the networks tends to forget the initial condition.

    abs(Δ) < 1e-12 && return 0.0

    if Δ == Δ0
         return ∫D(z -> ϕ(g * √Δ0 * z)^2)
    end

    A = min(abs(Δ), Δ0)
    a = √(max(Δ0 - A, 0.0))
    b = √A

    m(z) = ∫D(x -> ϕ(g * (a * x + b * z)))

    return sign(Δ) * ∫D(z -> m(z)^2)
end

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Potential V(Δ; Δ0)
# ------------------------------------------------------------

function V(Δ, Δ0, g)

    m1 = ∫D(x -> Φ(g * √Δ0 * x))

    if Δ == Δ0
        m1 = ∫D(z -> Φ(g * √Δ0 * z))
        m2 = ∫D(z -> Φ(g * √Δ0 * z)^2)
        
        return -0.5 * Δ0^2 + (m2 - m1^2) / g^2
    end

    A = min(abs(Δ), Δ0)
    a = √max(Δ0 - A, 0.0)
    b = √A

    m(z) = ∫D(x -> Φ(g * (a * x + b * z)))

    return - 0.5 * Δ^2 + (∫D(z -> m(z)^2) - m1^2) / g^2
end

# Curvature of the potential at the origin
function Vcurv0(Δ0, g)
    m = ∫D(x -> ∂ϕ(g * √Δ0 * x))
    return g^2 * m^2 - 1
end

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# The three phase-diagram curves
# ------------------------------------------------------------

# Boundary between single-well and double-well potential
function Δ0_boundary(g; Δmin = 1e-9, Δmax = 1.2)
    g < 1 && return NaN

    return find_zero(Δ0 -> Vcurv0(Δ0, g), (Δmin, Δmax), Bisection())
end

# Decaying solution: selects the initial condition such that Δ(τ) → 0 as τ → ∞
function Δ0_decay(g; Δmin = 1e-9, Δmax = 1.2)
    g < 1 && return NaN

    return find_zero(Δ0 -> V(Δ0, Δ0, g), (Δmin, Δmax), Bisection())
end

# Static, fixed point solution
function Δ0_static(g; Δmin=1e-9, Δmax = 1.2)
    g < 1 && return NaN

    return find_zero(Δ0 -> F(Δ0, Δ0, g) - Δ0, (Δmin, Δmax), Bisection())
end

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Numerical solution of Newton's equation:
#
# Δ'' = Δ - F(Δ; Δ0)
# ------------------------------------------------------------

function rhs!(du, u, p, t)
    Δ, v = u
    Δ0, g = p

    du[1] = v
    du[2] = Δ - F(Δ, Δ0, g)
end

function orbit(Δ0, g; T = 60.0, dt = 0.05)
    init_cond = [Δ0, 0.0]
    tspan = (0.0, T)
    params = (Δ0, g)
    prob = ODEProblem(rhs!, init_cond, tspan, params)
    return solve(prob, Tsit5(); saveat = dt, abstol = 1e-8, reltol = 1e-8)
end


end

We recap here also the three special values of Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​ given by the conditions:

V′′(0;Δ0)=0, V''(0;\Delta_0)=0, V′′(0;Δ0​)=0, V(Δ0;Δ0)=0, V(\Delta_0;\Delta_0)=0, V(Δ0​;Δ0​)=0, V′(Δ0;Δ0)=0 V'(\Delta_0; \Delta_0) = 0 %F(\Delta_0;\Delta_0)=\Delta_0. V′(Δ0​;Δ0​)=0

The first equation defines boundary between the single-well and double-well shapes; the second one selects the decaying solution Δ(τ)→0\Delta(\tau) \to 0Δ(τ)→0 for τ→∞\tau \to \inftyτ→∞ and finally the third one gives the static solution. Those three conditions are implemented respectively in the functions Δ0_boundary, Δ0_decay and Δ0_static in the julia code above.

What's next?

I will deliberately leave two questions for a second post. First, for g>1g>1g>1, the one-replica DMFT equation admits several formal solutions, and one has to understand which of them corresponds to a stable attractor of the original neural network dynamics. We will find out that the only physical DMFT solution is the decaying one. Secondly, one can ask whether this solution is genuinely chaotic.
