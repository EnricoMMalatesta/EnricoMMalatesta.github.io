The random minimum spanning tree problem

23 November 2026 | E. Malatesta

← All blog posts

Consider the complete graph KNK_NKN​ and assign an independent random variable weight LeL_eLe​ to every edge eee.

Let ENE_NEN​ be the total length of its minimum spanning tree (MST)

From a statistical-physics point of view, ENE_NEN​ is the ground-state energy of the spanning-tree problem. A beautiful result proved by Frieze [Frieze (1985)] states that, if the lengths LeL_eLe​ are uniform on [0,1][0,1][0,1], then

lim⁡N→∞E[EN]=ζ(3)=∑k=1∞1k3≃1.2020569. \lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb E[E_N] = \zeta(3) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^3} \simeq 1.2020569. N→∞lim​E[EN​]=ζ(3)=k=1∑∞​k31​≃1.2020569.

In this post I want to derive the result by translating Kruskal's algorithm into the language of the Erdős–Rényi random-graph process [Erdős and Rényi (1960)]. The logic is the following. Kruskal's algorithm first turns the optimal cost into an integral over the number of connected components of a threshold graph. At edge-length scale t/Nt/Nt/N, this threshold graph is the sparse Erdős–Rényi graph G(N,t/N)G(N,t/N)G(N,t/N). Its connected-component density can be written compactly in terms of the fraction u(t)u(t)u(t) of vertices outside the giant component. Finally, expanding the same density by the size of its finite trees produces one term 1/k31/k^31/k3 for each tree size kkk.

Kruskal's algorithm

Kruskal's algorithm [Kruskal (1956)] constructs an MST by sorting all the edges in increasing order of weight and then inspecting them one at a time. An edge is accepted if and only if its endpoints currently belong to two different connected components. If they are already connected, the edge is rejected because it would create a cycle. The algorithm stops after N−1N-1N−1 edges have been accepted.

The evolving set of accepted edges is therefore always a forest. Every accepted edge merges two of its connected components, and after N−1N-1N−1 successful mergers only one component remains.

The standard implementation sorts the MMM edges in O(Mlog⁡M)O(M\log M)O(MlogM) time and maintains the components with a disjoint-set, or union–find, data structure. The union–find operations take O(Mα(N))O(M\alpha(N))O(Mα(N)) time, where α\alphaα is the inverse Ackermann function, so sorting dominates. For the complete graph, M=(N2)M=\binom N2M=(2N​), and the total running time is

O(Mlog⁡M)=O(N2log⁡N). O(M\log M)=O(N^2\log N). O(MlogM)=O(N2logN).

The Erdős–Rényi random graph

We would like now to determine the expected number of connected components of an Erdős–Rényi random graph with edge probability scaling as ℓ=pN\ell = \frac{p}{N}ℓ=Np​. To do that, it is useful to recall some properties of the Erdős–Rényi random graph ensemble G(N,p/N)\mathcal{G}(N,p/N)G(N,p/N).

Note that the scaling ℓ=pN\ell = \frac{p}{N}ℓ=Np​ makes the probability of a vertex iii to have degree degi\mathrm{deg}_idegi​ equal to kkk Poissonian distributed in the large NNN limit. Indeed

P[degi=k]=(N−1k)(pN)k(1−pN)N−1−k →N→∞ e−ppkk!. P[\mathrm{deg}_i=k] = \binom{N-1}{k} \left(\frac{p}{N}\right)^k \left(1-\frac{p}{N}\right)^{N-1-k} \; \xrightarrow{N \to \infty} \; e^{-p}\frac{p^k}{k!}. P[degi​=k]=(kN−1​)(Np​)k(1−Np​)N−1−kN→∞​e−pk!pk​.

Note that therefore ppp represents the mean degree when NNN is large: p=E[degi]p = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{deg}_i]p=E[degi​].

Locally tree-like property

What does an Erdős–Rényi graph look like locally? In order to answer this question, we take a vertex iii at random, and we ask what is the probability that iii is belongs to a cycle ωs\omega_sωs​ of length s? To answer, let's start from iii; I have N−1N-1N−1 possibilities to connect with another node i1i_1i1​ and this happens with probability p/Np/Np/N. Then from i1i_1i1​ I can choose another vertex i2i_2i2​ in N−2N-2N−2 ways with probability p/Np/Np/N. I iterate this procedure until I get to node is−1i_{s-1}is−1​ after which I have to come back to vertex iii. We therefore have

P[i∈ωs]=12(N−1)pN(N−2)pN…(N−s+1)pN⋅pN≃ps2N \begin{aligned} P[i \in \omega_s] &= \frac{1}{2} (N-1) \frac{p}{N} (N-2) \frac{p}{N} \dots (N-s+1) \frac{p}{N} \cdot \frac{p}{N} \simeq \frac{p^s}{2N} \end{aligned} P[i∈ωs​]​=21​(N−1)Np​(N−2)Np​…(N−s+1)Np​⋅Np​≃2Nps​​

where the factor 1/21/21/2 is added to avoid double counting (since the loop can be travelled starting from iii in two possible directions). This probability goes to zero in the limit of large NNN. This means that if one takes a random node iii and looks to a finite neighborhood sss with high probability we will not see any loop. This property is called tree-like property of the Erdős–Rényi random graph. From this also follows that the expected number of loops of length sss

E[Ls]≃ps2NNs=ps2s \mathbb{E} [\mathcal{L}_s] \simeq \frac{p^s}{2N} \frac{N}{s} = \frac{p^s}{2s} E[Ls​]≃2Nps​sN​=2sps​

is of order 1 and does not scale with NNN. The factor N/sN/sN/s has a simple origin. Multiplying by NNN sums over the possible choices of a marked vertex iii. In this way, however, every cycle of length sss is counted sss times, once for each of its vertices. We must therefore divide by sss. For p<1p<1p<1, equation (12) shows that E[Ls]\mathbb E[\mathcal L_s]E[Ls​] decreases exponentially with sss, so cycles are rare and a vertex chosen uniformly at random does not belong to any loop.

A similar argument also determines the typical component size. Starting from vertex iii, on average it has ppp neighbors; each of them has, on average, ppp neighbours. For p<1p<1p<1 one therefore gets

E[∣C(i)∣]≃∑s=0∞ps=11−p, \mathbb E[|\mathcal C(i)|]\simeq\sum_{s=0}^{\infty} p^s = \frac{1}{1-p}, E[∣C(i)∣]≃s=0∑∞​ps=1−p1​,

so a typical vertex belongs to a finite component of size O(1)O(1)O(1), which is a tree with high probability. For p>1p>1p>1, the exploration may instead continue growing as prp^rpr. When p>1p>1p>1 instead, the size of the connected component scales as psp^sps; when this quantity becomes of order NNN loops can appear. The typical length of a loop when p>1p>1p>1 is therefore

s≃ln⁡Nln⁡p s \simeq \frac{\ln N}{\ln p} s≃lnplnN​

The equation for the giant component

The exploration argument above suggests a qualitative change at p=1p=1p=1. For p<1p<1p<1, the number of newly discovered vertices decreases on average and the exploration eventually stops, producing a finite component. For p>1p>1p>1, it grows on average and has a positive probability of reaching an extensive number of nodes of the graph. In the large-NNN limit, this produces a unique component containing order-NNN vertices, known as the giant component. We now determine the fraction of vertices belonging to it.

Let us denote by u(p)u(p)u(p) the fraction of vertices not belonging to the giant component. For a vertex iii not to belong to the giant component, it must not be connected to any other vertex that belongs to the giant component. For every vertex j≠ij\ne ij=i, one of the following must occur:

  • vertex jjj does not belong to the neighborhood of node iii, i.e. j∉∂ij \notin \partial ij∈/∂i. This event happens with probability 1−pN1-\frac{p}{N}1−Np​;

  • j∈∂ij \in \partial ij∈∂i, but jjj is not in the giant component; this event has probability pNu\frac{p}{N} uNp​u.

The total probability that iii does not belong to the giant component is therefore 1−pN+pNu1- \frac{p}{N} + \frac{p}{N} u1−Np​+Np​u. For iii to be disconnected from the giant component this must be true for all the N−1N-1N−1 possible choices of jjj, so that[1]

u=(1−p(1−u)N)N−1 →N→∞ u=e−p(1−u). u = \left(1 - \frac{p(1-u)}{N}\right)^{N-1} \; \xrightarrow{N\to\infty} \; u = e^{-p(1-u)}. u=(1−Np(1−u)​)N−1N→∞​u=e−p(1−u).

For p>1p>1p>1, a nonzero giant component emerges continuously, with uuu determined by u=e−p(1−u)u=e^{-p(1-u)}u=e−p(1−u).

The graph outside the giant component

In the previous section we have found how the giant component emerges. However, this does not count in the number of connected components CN(ℓ)\mathcal{C}_N(\ell)CN​(ℓ) when NNN is large (recall equation (9)). We therefore need to examine how typically an Erdős–Rényi random graph looks like for vertices not belonging to the giant component.

Choose uniformly at random a vertex iii not belonging to the giant component i∉gci \notin \mathrm{gc}i∈/gc. We would like to compute what is the probability that it has degree kkk. For iii to remain outside the giant component, given it has degree kkk, it must be that none of its kkk neighbouring branches reaches the giant component. Each branch is not in the giant component with probability uuu, and therefore

P[i∉gc∣degi=k]=uk. P[i \notin \mathrm{gc} \mid \mathrm{deg}_i=k]=u^k. P[i∈/gc∣degi​=k]=uk.

Using Bayes' rule, we obtain

P[degi=k∣i∉gc]=P[i∉gc∣degi=k]P[degi=k]P[gc]=ukue−ppkk!. \begin{aligned} P[\mathrm{deg}_i=k \mid i \notin \mathrm{gc}] &= \frac{ P[i \notin \mathrm{gc} \mid \mathrm{deg}_i=k] P[\mathrm{deg}_i=k] }{ P[\mathrm{gc}] }\\ &= \frac{u^k}{u} e^{-p}\frac{p^k}{k!}. \end{aligned} P[degi​=k∣i∈/gc]​=P[gc]P[i∈/gc∣degi​=k]P[degi​=k]​=uuk​e−pk!pk​.​

The self-consistency equation (15) implies

e−pu=e−pu. \frac{e^{-p}}{u}=e^{-pu}. ue−p​=e−pu.

Hence when we condition on a random vertex iii not belonging to the giant component the probability it has degree equal to kkk

P[degi=k∣i∉gc]=e−pu(pu)kk!. P[\mathrm{deg}_i=k \mid i \notin \mathrm{gc}] = e^{-pu}\frac{(pu)^k}{k!}. P[degi​=k∣i∈/gc]=e−puk!(pu)k​.

is still Poissonian but with a mean degree pupupu. Crucially, this effective mean is smaller than one. Taking the logarithm of the equation (15) gives

p=−log⁡u1−u ⟹ pu=−ulog⁡u1−u. p=\frac{-\log u}{1-u} \implies pu=\frac{-u\log u}{1-u}. p=1−u−logu​⟹pu=1−u−ulogu​.

For 0<u<10 < u < 10<u<1,

−ulog⁡u=∫u1ux dx<∫u1dx=1−u. -u\log u = \int_u^1\frac{u}{x}\,dx < \int_u^1 dx = 1-u. −ulogu=∫u1​xu​dx<∫u1​dx=1−u.

from which it follows that pu<1pu <1pu<1.

Thus, although the original graph is in the supercritical phase p>1p>1p>1, the graph outside the giant component (composed by uNuNuN vertices) behaves asymptotically as an Erdős–Rényi random graph in the subcritical q<1q<1q<1 phase:

G(uN,pN)=G(uN,quN),q≡pu<1. \mathcal{G}\left(uN,\frac{p}{N}\right) = \mathcal{G}\left(uN,\frac{q}{uN}\right), \qquad q\equiv pu<1. G(uN,Np​)=G(uN,uNq​),q≡pu<1.

This property is known as the duality principle.

We can summarize the evolution of G(N,p/N)\mathcal{G}(N,p/N)G(N,p/N) as ppp increases as follows:

  • for p<1p<1p<1, a vertex chosen at random is located in a tree with high probability; it is possible to show that the largest components have size O(log⁡N)O(\log N)O(logN);

  • at p=1p=1p=1, it is possible to show that the largest components have size O(N2/3)O(N^{2/3})O(N2/3);

  • for p>1p>1p>1, a unique giant component of size (1−u)N(1-u)N(1−u)N appears, while the graph outside the giant is effectively subcritical, with mean degree pu<1pu<1pu<1. Therefore a uniformly chosen vertex, conditioned on lying outside the giant component, belongs to a tree component with high probability;

  • when ppp is of order log⁡N\log NlogN the graph becomes connected.

Counting the number of trees

From the analysis of the previous section we found that the number of connected components of an Erdős–Rényi random graph is dominated by trees. Now we will compute explicitly the number of tree components. Let TN,kT_{N,k}TN,k​ be the number of tree components containing exactly kkk vertices in G(N,pN)\mathcal{G}\left(N,\frac{p}{N}\right)G(N,Np​). We can count the expected value of TN,kT_{N,k}TN,k​ by the following reasoning.

  • Choose kkk vertices out of the NNN available; there are (Nk)\binom Nk(kN​) possibilities;

  • From those kkk vertices Cayley's formula tells us that we can construct kk−2k^{k-2}kk−2 spanning trees;

  • Then we have to require that the k−1k-1k−1 edges of out kkk vertex tree are present. This happens with probability (pN)k−1\left(\frac{p}{N}\right)^{k-1}(Np​)k−1;

  • Next we have to assure that no other edges are present between our kkk vertices: this event has probability (1−pN)(k2)−(k−1)\left(1-\frac{p}{N}\right)^{\binom{k}{2}-(k-1)}(1−Np​)(2k​)−(k−1);

  • Finally we have to impose that there are no edges between these kkk vertices and the remaining N−kN-kN−k: this gives an additional factor (1−pN)k(N−k)\left(1-\frac{p}{N}\right)^{k(N-k)}(1−Np​)k(N−k)

Therefore, putting all the mentioned factors together, we have

E[TN,k]=(Nk)kk−2(pN)k−1(1−pN)k(N−k)+(k2)−(k−1). \mathbb E[T_{N, k}] = \binom Nk k^{k-2} \left(\frac{p}{N}\right)^{k-1} \left(1-\frac{p}{N}\right)^{ k(N-k)+\binom{k}{2}-(k-1) }. E[TN,k​]=(kN​)kk−2(Np​)k−1(1−Np​)k(N−k)+(2k​)−(k−1).

For fixed kkk and large NNN, we obtain

E[TN,k]≃Nkk!kk−2(pN)k−1e−kp=Nkk−2k!pk−1e−kp \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[T_{N, k}] &\simeq \frac{N^k}{k!} k^{k-2} \left(\frac{p}{N}\right)^{k-1} e^{-k p} \\ &=N \frac{k^{k-2}}{k!} p^{k-1} e^{-k p} \end{aligned} E[TN,k​]​≃k!Nk​kk−2(Np​)k−1e−kp=Nk!kk−2​pk−1e−kp​

Summing over kkk and dividing by NNN we finally get

κ(p)=∑k≥1kk−2k!pk−1e−kp. \boxed{ \kappa(p) = \sum_{k\ge1} \frac{k^{k-2}}{k!} p^{k-1}e^{-kp}. } κ(p)=k≥1∑​k!kk−2​pk−1e−kp.​
